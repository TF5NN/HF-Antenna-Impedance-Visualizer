Model notes & derivations

Why the graph starts at λ/4 of the lowest band

A wire shorter than one quarter-wavelength (λ/4) can radiate, but it is "electrically short": radiation resistance falls roughly as (L/λ)², while capacitive reactance becomes very large. The result is very low efficiency and a feed impedance that is almost impossible to match without special loading coils. The open-circuit transmission-line model used here also becomes inaccurate below λ/4. Hiding this region keeps the graph focused on practically useful end-fed wire lengths.

Characteristic impedance Z₀ = 550 Ω

The transmission-line formula |Z| = Z₀ · |cot(βL)| requires a characteristic impedance for the wire. For a single wire above ground, Z₀ depends on wire diameter and height: Z₀ ≈ 138 · log₁₀(4h / d) where h is height above ground and d is wire diameter. For a typical 20 AWG (~0.8 mm) wire at 6–10 m height this gives roughly 500–600 Ω; 550 Ω is used here as a representative mid-range value. Thicker wire (e.g. 14 AWG, ~1.6 mm) lowers Z₀ by ~30–50 Ω; greater height increases it by a similar amount. The model is calibrated for typical portable and backyard installations — not low-height DX antennas or elevated ground-plane verticals, where Z₀ may differ significantly.

Physics model — lossy open-circuit transmission line

The antenna wire is modelled as an open-ended lossy transmission line with complex propagation constant γ = α + jβ. The feedpoint impedance is:

Z = Z₀ · coth(γL) = Z₀ · [sinh(2αL) + j·sin(2βL)] / [cosh(2αL) − cos(2βL)]

This gives a complex result {R, X} at every wire length. At λ/2 resonance both sin(2βL) and the denominator are small, so R ≈ Z₀/(αL) ≈ 3 000 Ω and X ≈ 0 — the antenna is nearly purely resistive and a 49:1 unun gives SWR ≈ 1.2:1. At λ/4 anti-resonance sin(2βL) = 0 again, so X = 0 and R ≈ Z₀·αL ≈ 34 Ω — matching directly with a 1:1 choke. Between resonances the large reactive component X drives SWR well above 2:1 through any practical transformer, so matching zones are naturally narrow (~±1.5 m around each resonance for 80m).

Parameters: Z₀ = 450 Ω (characteristic impedance, typical for HF wire near ground). α = 0.003 75 Np/m (attenuation constant, representing radiation + ohmic loss; calibrated so the 80m EFHW peak is ≈ 3 000 Ω). The 5 000 Ω ceiling (Z_PEAK) clamps the R display at the top of the Y axis.

Why the impedance floor is 25 Ω

At anti-resonance (λ/4 wire lengths), current is maximum at the feed point, giving low impedance. A theoretically lossless wire would approach 0 Ω, but real wires at typical HF heights give roughly 20–60 Ω due to radiation resistance and conductor/ground losses. 25 Ω is a conservative educational floor that reflects real-world measurement data.

Velocity factor

Real wire is electrically shorter than its physical length — the RF wave travels at VF × c along the conductor, so resonant peaks shift left to VF × λ/2 physical length. Typical values: bare copper ~0.98, insulated antenna wire ~0.95–0.97. At VF = 0.975 the 40m EFHW resonance moves from 20.97 m (free-space) to ~20.4 m — matching real-world experience. The velocity factor slider applies equally to the main wire and any counterpoise.

Counterpoise effect

A counterpoise wire connects to the "cold" terminal of the transformer, providing an RF return path. In the model both wires are open-ended lossy TLs whose complex impedances add in series at the feedpoint: Z_feed = Z_wire(L_wire) + Z_cp(L_cp). A counterpoise at λ/4 contributes mainly resistive impedance (low, ~34 Ω) — little effect on a 49:1 match. One near λ/2 contributes high resistive + near-zero reactive impedance and raises the total feedpoint Z, which can pull it out of the matching zone. When enabled, dashed ghost curves show the wire-alone impedance for comparison.

Model limitations: This is a qualitative guide, not a precise predictor. Key simplifications: (1) No mutual coupling — the wire and counterpoise radiate into each other's near field; omitting this can produce ±50% errors in the combined value. (2) No ground interaction — a real counterpoise near or on the ground has capacitive coupling to soil that shifts its resonant length and lowers its effective characteristic impedance. (3) Same Z₀ and α for both conductors — the counterpoise may differ significantly if it is shorter, thicker, or near ground. Treat the counterpoise display as a qualitative guide; for quantitative results use a NEC2/4 simulator.

Matching efficiency — how much power reaches the antenna

Efficiency has two parts: mismatch loss (power reflected at the impedance boundary) and network loss (heat in the transformer/tuner). The tooltip shows the combined estimate when the antenna falls inside a zone.

Mismatch loss: η = 1 − |Γ|² where Γ = (Z − Zref) / (Z + Zref) uses the full complex impedance Z = R + jX. At perfect match (Z = Zref, purely resistive) η = 100%. At SWR 2:1 η ≈ 89% — only ~11% is reflected. Reactance increases |Γ| and worsens mismatch loss even when |Z| looks reasonable, which is why zones now use complex SWR.

Network loss estimates used: 1:1 Unun — modelled as lossless (η_net = 100%) because the choke passes RF essentially unchanged; real ferrite chokes have ~1–3% insertion loss, negligible for this purpose. 9:1 + Tuner — ~13% loss (transformer ~5%, tuner ~8%); 49:1 Unun — ~10% loss (ferrite core heating). These are broad estimates; real losses vary with power, frequency, core material, and winding quality.

Radiation efficiency is not modelled. The percentages shown describe how much transmitter power reaches the antenna feedpoint — they say nothing about how much of that power is actually radiated into space. Radiation efficiency depends on the ratio of radiation resistance to total loss resistance (conductor + ground + near-field), and varies enormously with installation. Rough ballparks: at EFHW resonance (49:1 zone) a well-installed wire is typically 70–90 % efficient because radiation resistance is high (hundreds to thousands of ohms) and dominates over losses. At λ/4 anti-resonance (1:1 Unun zone, ~25–50 Ω) radiation resistance is only ~10–20 Ω — comparable to wire and ground losses — giving 30–60 % in practice, despite the near-perfect impedance match. A high matching efficiency figure at a low-impedance length should not be interpreted as a high-performance antenna.

Sweet Spots overlay

The ★ Sweet Spots toggle highlights wire lengths where a minimum number of active bands simultaneously fall inside matching zones. A gold column appears wherever the condition is met; a ★ marks the centre of each cluster. The min bands control sets the threshold — it auto-defaults to the number of active bands, requiring all selected bands to coincide simultaneously.

This is particularly useful for identifying a single wire length that covers multiple bands without retuning — the classic goal of a multi-band EFHW. Note that sweet spots depend on which zones are toggled on: enabling only the 49:1 Unun zone will show lengths where two or more bands are simultaneously near half-wave resonance, which is a strong multi-band indicator; enabling all zones will show a wider (but less selective) set of lengths.

ITU Region 1 allocation width

The ITU R1 alloc. width toggle scales each band's curve thickness proportionally to the ITU Region 1 amateur radio frequency allocation for that band, using a logarithmic scale from 1 px (60m — 15 kHz) to 4 px (6m — 2000 kHz). A wider line means more frequency room: e.g. 10m (1700 kHz) is nearly as thick as 6m, while 30m (50 kHz) and 17m/12m (100 kHz each) are visibly thinner. This gives a quick sense of operating flexibility at any given wire length.

How the matching zone ranges were derived

1:1 Unun (25–100 Ω): A 1:1 unun (UNbalanced–UNbalanced) presents the same impedance on both ports — it does not transform the ratio, only suppresses common-mode RF current flowing back down the coax shield. In practice this is a current choke: a coil of coax, or coax threaded through ferrite cores (e.g. the W2DU choke). Because both the transmitter output and an end-fed wire are unbalanced, a unun is the correct term. It is commonly — and incorrectly — called a "1:1 balun"; a true balun (BALanced–UNbalanced) is only appropriate when one port is balanced, such as the centre of a dipole. A 50 Ω transceiver typically tolerates SWR ≤ 2:1, spanning 50 / 2 = 25 Ω to 50 × 2 = 100 Ω.

9:1 Unun + Tuner (75–1 800 Ω): A 9:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to ~450 Ω at the antenna. A typical L-network tuner can then handle roughly 75–1 800 Ω (approximately ÷6 to ×4 of the 450 Ω centre). The range is asymmetric because L-network topology inherently covers a wider ratio in one direction; a T-network tuner may extend further.

49:1 Unun (1 500–5 000 Ω): A 49:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to 50 × 49 = 2 450 Ω ideal. SWR ≤ 2:1 spans 2 450 / 2 = 1 225 Ω to 2 450 × 2 = 4 900 Ω. The zone shown starts at 1 500 Ω (a conservative lower bound — 1 225 Ω at SWR 2:1 is attainable but near the noisy anti-resonance region) and extends to 5 000 Ω, matching the model's impedance ceiling. At 5 000 Ω the SWR is 5 000 / 2 450 ≈ 2.0:1, right at the tolerance boundary.
