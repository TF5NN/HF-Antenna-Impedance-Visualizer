Model notes & derivations

Why the graph starts at λ/4 of the lowest band

A wire shorter than one quarter-wavelength (λ/4) can radiate, but it is "electrically short": radiation resistance falls roughly as (L/λ)², while capacitive reactance becomes very large. The result is very low efficiency and a feed impedance that is almost impossible to match without special loading coils. The open-circuit transmission-line model used here also becomes inaccurate below λ/4. Hiding this region keeps the graph focused on practically useful end-fed wire lengths.

Why the impedance ceiling is 5 000 Ω

The formula |Z| = Z₀ · |cot(βL)| gives Z → ∞ at exact half-wave resonances. In practice, radiation resistance and wire/ground losses limit the real peak to roughly 2 000–5 000 Ω depending on wire height and diameter. 5 000 Ω is the realistic upper bound used here. Values above it are clamped so the graph stays readable and physically plausible.

Why the impedance floor is 25 Ω

At anti-resonance (λ/4 wire lengths), current is maximum at the feed point, giving low impedance. A theoretically lossless wire would approach 0 Ω, but real wires at typical HF heights give roughly 20–60 Ω due to radiation resistance and conductor/ground losses. 25 Ω is a conservative educational floor that reflects real-world measurement data.

Velocity factor

Real wire is electrically shorter than its physical length — the RF wave travels at VF × c along the conductor, so resonant peaks shift left to VF × λ/2 physical length. Typical values: bare copper ~0.98, insulated antenna wire ~0.95–0.97. At VF = 0.975 the 40m EFHW resonance moves from 20.97 m (free-space) to ~20.4 m — matching real-world experience. The velocity factor slider applies equally to the main wire and any counterpoise.

Counterpoise effect

A counterpoise wire connects to the "cold" terminal of the transformer, providing an RF return path. In the lossless model both wires are purely reactive and add in series at the feedpoint: Z_feed = −jZ₀·cot(βL_wire) + −jZ₀·cot(βL_cp). The signs matter — a counterpoise at λ/4 has near-zero reactance (adds little), while one near λ/2 adds very high reactance and can completely ruin the match. When enabled, dashed ghost curves show the wire-alone impedance for direct comparison with the solid counterpoise-adjusted curves.

Model limitations: This is a qualitative guide, not a precise predictor. Four significant simplifications are made: (1) No mutual coupling — the wire and counterpoise radiate into each other's near field; omitting this mutual impedance term can produce ±50% errors in the combined value. (2) No ground interaction — a real counterpoise near or on the ground has capacitive coupling to soil that shifts its resonant length and lowers its effective characteristic impedance. (3) Pure reactance only — the counterpoise also has radiation resistance; that extracted power does not appear as added reactance and the model ignores it. (4) Same Z₀ for both conductors — 550 Ω is calibrated for the main wire at typical heights; the counterpoise may differ. For quantitative results use a NEC2/4 simulator.

Matching efficiency — how much power reaches the antenna

Efficiency has two parts: mismatch loss (power reflected at the impedance boundary) and network loss (heat in the transformer/tuner). The tooltip shows the combined estimate when the antenna falls inside a zone.

Mismatch loss: η = 4·Z·Zref / (Z + Zref)² where Zref is the transformer's design impedance. At perfect match η = 100%. At SWR 2:1 (Z = 2·Zref) η ≈ 89% — only ~11% is reflected. Even a poor match within a zone wastes little power as mismatch alone; network loss often matters more.

Network loss estimates used: Direct 50 Ω — none (Zref = 50 Ω, η_net = 100%); 9:1 + Tuner — ~13% loss (transformer ~5%, tuner ~8%); 49:1 Unun — ~10% loss (ferrite core heating). These are broad estimates; real losses vary with power, frequency, core material, and winding quality.

How the matching zone ranges were derived

Direct 50 Ω (25–100 Ω): A 50 Ω transceiver typically tolerates SWR ≤ 2:1. SWR 2:1 spans 50 / 2 = 25 Ω to 50 × 2 = 100 Ω.

9:1 Unun + Tuner (75–1 800 Ω): A 9:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to ~450 Ω at the antenna. A good tuner extends the match by roughly ±4×, giving 450 / 6 ≈ 75 Ω to 450 × 4 ≈ 1 800 Ω.

49:1 Unun (1 500–4 500 Ω): A 49:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to 50 × 49 = 2 450 Ω ideal. SWR ≤ 2:1 spans 2 450 / 2 = 1 225 Ω to 2 450 × 2 = 4 900 Ω. Real antenna peaks reach 2 000–5 000 Ω depending on height — even 4 500 Ω gives only ~1.8:1 SWR, well within tolerance. The zone shown (1 500–4 500 Ω) is the practical usable range.
