Model notes & derivations

Graph X-axis range

The Start and End drop-downs below the graph control the wire-length range displayed. Default start is ⅛ λ of the highest active band — short enough to show the first matching opportunities without wasting space on electrically tiny wires. Default end is 1.25 × λ/2 of the lowest active band, which places the lowest-band half-wave resonance peak (the main 49:1 Unun target) roughly three-quarters of the way across the X axis, leaving a comfortable tail to the right.

Wires shorter than ⅛ λ are not useful in practice: radiation resistance falls as (L/λ)², capacitive reactance becomes very large, and the transmission-line model used here is least accurate in this region. Start the graph at a short distance only if you are specifically studying loading-coil or matching-network designs for short verticals.

Wire impedance Z₀

The wire is treated as a transmission line whose average characteristic impedance depends on how thin it is compared with the wavelength: Z₀ = 60 · ln(0.22 · λ / a) · VF, with a the wire radius. The tool assumes 1 mm diameter antenna wire, which gives about 590 Ω on 40 m and 510 Ω on 10 m for bare wire. Z₀ sets how quickly reactance grows away from resonance, and together with the radiation resistance it sets the height of the half-wave peaks.

Physics model — line loss equals radiated power

Each conductor is an open-ended transmission line with feedpoint impedance

Z = Z₀ · coth(a + jθ)

where θ is the electrical length. The loss term a is not a fitted number: it is chosen so the line dissipates exactly the power the wire radiates, tanh(a) = R_rad / Z₀. R_rad is the classic induced-EMF radiation resistance of a sinusoidal standing-wave current, referred to the current maximum.

At current-fed lengths (λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4 …) this gives R ≈ R_rad: about 36 Ω, 53 Ω, 60 Ω. At voltage-fed lengths (λ/2, λ …) it gives R ≈ Z₀² / R_rad: about 3.4 kΩ for a 40 m half-wave, falling slowly on higher harmonics. In between, the standing wave supplies the reactance — capacitive below λ/4, inductive from λ/4 to λ/2 — which is what keeps the matching zones narrow.

Harmonics are not exact multiples. An open wire end adds a fixed electrical length, not a percentage of the whole wire: about 0.8 % of a half-wave on 160 m, 1.2 % on 40 m and 1.7 % on 10 m for 1 mm wire (the thicker the wire is compared with the wavelength, the larger it gets). The n-th half-wave point is therefore at (n − end) · VF · λ/2, so a wire cut for 40 m is slightly long on 20, 15 and 10 m — the familiar end-fed half-wave behaviour.

Display limits: R is clamped at 5 000 Ω and the plotted |Z| at 25 Ω.

Why the plotted floor is 25 Ω

At quarter-wave lengths current is maximum at the feedpoint and the impedance is low. A single wire against an ideal ground bottoms out at about 36 Ω in this model; a wire with a counterpoise can go lower or higher. 25 Ω is only the bottom of the plotted range (SWR 2:1 on a direct 50 Ω feed) — real installations add ground and conductor loss on top.

Velocity factor

The slider is the slowing caused by the wire's insulation only: bare wire 1.00, thin PVC about 0.97–0.99, thick insulation 0.94–0.97. The shortening caused by the open wire end is handled separately (see the physics note). At the default 0.975 a 40 m end-fed half-wave comes out at about 20.2 m. The velocity factor applies equally to the main wire and any counterpoise.

Counterpoise effect

A counterpoise connects to the "cold" terminal of the transformer. Wire and counterpoise are then the two arms of one antenna, in series across the feed: Z_feed = Z_wire + Z_cp, including the coupling between the two (the same model as the dipole mode fed off-centre). Without a counterpoise the wire is modelled against an ideal ground. When enabled, dashed ghost curves show that wire-alone impedance for comparison.

Model limitations: the counterpoise is assumed straight, in line with the wire and in free space. A counterpoise lying on the ground, or a coax shield acting as one, is detuned and lossier than this. Treat the display as a guide.

Dipole and off-centre feed

The generator sits between the two arms, so their impedances add: Z_feed = Z_A + Z_B. A centre-fed half-wave comes out at about 73 Ω (free space). A resonant wire stays resonant wherever it is fed, and the resistance rises as R(p) ≈ R_centre / sin²(π·p): about 100 Ω at 33 %, 140 Ω at 25 % on the fundamental. On harmonics the pattern repeats along the wire, which is why an off-centre feed can find 200–300 Ω on several bands at once.

Height above ground (optional, off by default): the ground acts as a partial mirror, so a horizontal wire also "sees" an upside-down copy of itself. That changes how much it radiates and therefore its resistance: a half-wave dipole reads about 45 Ω at 0.12 λ, rises to about 80 Ω near a quarter-wave up and settles towards 73 Ω when high. The tool assumes average soil (εr 13, 5 mS/m) and a straight horizontal wire. The height is in metres, so every band sees a different electrical height. The small shift in resonant length that the ground also causes is not modelled.

How accurate is it?

The model was compared with NEC-2 for 1 mm bare wire on 40, 20, 15 and 10 m. Typical (RMS) error in |Z|: about 4 % for an end-fed wire against ideal ground, 12 % for off-centre-fed wires, 11 % for an end-fed wire with a counterpoise and 13 % for a dipole 5–20 m above average ground. Method and numbers are in validation/nec_comparison_v3.md.

Not modelled: ground under an end-fed wire, sloping or bent wires, nearby objects, feedline common-mode current and the transformer itself. Real 49:1 transformers often work poorly on 10 m because of their own stray capacitance and leakage; that is the transformer, not the wire. These need a full simulator such as NEC, or measurement.

Matching efficiency — how much power reaches the antenna

The tooltip ~xx% figure is the total delivered-power efficiency: η_total = η_mismatch × η_transformer × η_tuner

Mismatch efficiency (no tuner, or no tuner match): η_mismatch = 1 − |Γ|² where Γ = (Za − 50) / (Za + 50) and Za is the impedance presented to the radio side of the transformer. Full complex arithmetic is used — reactance increases |Γ| even when |Z| looks numerically close to the reference.

Transformer efficiency is a fixed constant per zone based on measured ferrite-toroid insertion loss at HF: 1:1 choke — 100% (negligible loss); 9:1 Unun — 90%; 49:1 Unun — 90%. Real losses vary with power level, frequency, and core material.

Tuner efficiency (when an ATU is enabled) is computed on the solved circuit: each inductor gets a loss resistance X_L / Q with Q = 120, capacitors are treated as lossless, and the efficiency is the power reaching the load divided by the power entering the network. Hard matches (large impedance ratio or a lot of reactance to cancel) need more inductance and lose more. When the tuner finds a match the radio sees 50 Ω, so no mismatch loss remains: the figure is then η_transformer × η_tuner. The tooltip's "Q≈" is the loaded Q of the match. When no ATU is active, η_tuner = 100%.

Radiation efficiency is not modelled. The percentage shown describes power delivered to the feedpoint, not power radiated. Radiation efficiency depends on radiation resistance vs total loss resistance (conductor + ground + near-field) and varies enormously with installation. At EFHW resonance (49:1 zone) a well-installed wire is typically 70–90% efficient; at λ/4 anti-resonance (1:1 zone, ~25–50 Ω) radiation resistance is only ~10–20 Ω — comparable to losses — giving 30–60% despite a near-perfect impedance match.

ATU L-network solver

When an Antenna Tuner is enabled, the tool runs an analytic L-network solver at each point in the tooltip. Two topologies are tried in order: series→shunt (series element facing the antenna, shunt element to ground) and shunt→series. The winning topology, inductance (µH), and capacitance (pF) are shown in the tooltip, along with the inductor-Q efficiency estimate. If neither topology can reach a match within the preset's Lmax/Cmax limits, "No match within tuner limits" is shown and the zone does not light up.

ATU presets — Internal (3:1): Lmax 4 µH, Cmax 500 pF; External (10:1): Lmax 24 µH, Cmax 1 200 pF; Wide-range (20:1): Lmax 60 µH, Cmax 3 500 pF. A Custom mode lets you set all three parameters manually.

Sweet Spots overlay

The ★ Sweet Spots toggle highlights wire lengths where a minimum number of active bands simultaneously fall inside matching zones. A gold column appears wherever the condition is met; a ★ marks the centre of each cluster. The min bands control sets the threshold — it auto-defaults to the number of active bands, requiring all selected bands to coincide simultaneously.

This is particularly useful for identifying a single wire length that covers multiple bands without retuning — the classic goal of a multi-band EFHW. By default every counted band must match through one and the same transformer, so a listed length is something you can build without swapping transformers; each transformer is searched separately and the table says which one a row belongs to. Turn on Mix transformers to count a band when any of the selected transformers matches it. Note that sweet spots depend on which zones are toggled on: enabling only the 49:1 Unun zone will show lengths where two or more bands are simultaneously near half-wave resonance, which is a strong multi-band indicator; enabling all zones will show a wider (but less selective) set of lengths.

ITU Region 1 allocation width

The ITU R1 alloc. width toggle scales each band's curve thickness proportionally to the ITU Region 1 amateur radio frequency allocation for that band, using a logarithmic scale from 1 px (60m — 15 kHz) to 4 px (6m — 2000 kHz). A wider line means more frequency room: e.g. 10m (1700 kHz) is nearly as thick as 6m, while 30m (50 kHz) and 17m/12m (100 kHz each) are visibly thinner. This gives a quick sense of operating flexibility at any given wire length.

How the matching zone ranges were derived

Zone overlay bands are dynamic — they grow or shrink based on tuner state and selected ATU preset:

1:1 Unun (25–100 Ω): A 1:1 unun (UNbalanced–UNbalanced) presents the same impedance on both ports — it does not transform the ratio, only suppresses common-mode RF current flowing back down the coax shield. In practice this is a current choke: a coil of coax, or coax threaded through ferrite cores (e.g. the W2DU choke). Because both the transmitter output and an end-fed wire are unbalanced, a unun is the correct term. It is commonly — and incorrectly — called a "1:1 balun"; a true balun (BALanced–UNbalanced) is only appropriate when one port is balanced, such as the centre of a dipole. A 50 Ω transceiver typically tolerates SWR ≤ 2:1, spanning 50 / 2 = 25 Ω to 50 × 2 = 100 Ω.

9:1 Unun (225–900 Ω without tuner): A 9:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to ~450 Ω at the antenna (zRef = 450 Ω). Without a tuner the overlay shows the natural SWR ≤ 2:1 window: 450 / 2 = 225 Ω to 450 × 2 = 900 Ω. When an ATU is active the overlay expands to the tuner preset's maxSWR (e.g. external 10:1 → ~45–4 500 Ω) reflecting the wider range the combined system can handle. Between wire resonances the feedpoint always has a large reactive component X, so complex SWR through the 9:1 is typically 7–15:1 even when |Z| ≈ 450 Ω — this is why the zone appears narrow without a tuner and why a counterpoise (3–7 m) that partially cancels the reactance is effectively part of the matching system.

49:1 Unun (1 225–4 900 Ω, shown as ~1 225–5 000 Ω): A 49:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to 50 × 49 = 2 450 Ω ideal (zRef). SWR ≤ 2:1 spans 2 450 / 2 = 1 225 Ω to 2 450 × 2 = 4 900 Ω. The overlay extends to the 5 000 Ω model ceiling, which is within measurement noise of the theoretical boundary. When a tuner is on the band widens to the preset's maxSWR, allowing the ATU to absorb residual mismatch at higher-order harmonics through the 49:1 zone.
