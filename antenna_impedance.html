Model notes & derivations

Graph X-axis range

The Start and End drop-downs below the graph control the wire-length range displayed. Default start is ⅛ λ of the highest active band — short enough to show the first matching opportunities without wasting space on electrically tiny wires. Default end is 1.25 × λ/2 of the lowest active band, which places the lowest-band half-wave resonance peak (the main 49:1 Unun target) roughly three-quarters of the way across the X axis, leaving a comfortable tail to the right.

Wires shorter than ⅛ λ are not useful in practice: radiation resistance falls as (L/λ)², capacitive reactance becomes very large, and the transmission-line model used here is least accurate in this region. Start the graph at a short distance only if you are specifically studying loading-coil or matching-network designs for short verticals.

Characteristic impedance Z₀ = 450 Ω

The transmission-line model requires a characteristic impedance for the wire. For a single wire above ground, Z₀ depends on wire diameter and height: Z₀ ≈ 138 · log₁₀(4h / d) where h is height above ground and d is wire diameter. For a typical 20 AWG (~0.8 mm) wire at 6–10 m height this gives roughly 500–600 Ω; the model uses 450 Ω as a conservative midpoint that also accounts for the effective impedance averaging that occurs in sloped or bent installations. The attenuation constant α is then calibrated so that the λ/2 resonance peak is approximately 3 000 Ω — consistent with published EFHW measurements.

Physics model — lossy open-circuit transmission line

The antenna wire is modelled as an open-ended lossy transmission line with complex propagation constant γ = α + jβ. The feedpoint impedance is:

Z = Z₀ · coth(γL) = Z₀ · [sinh(2αL) + j·sin(2βL)] / [cosh(2αL) − cos(2βL)]

This gives a complex result {R, X} at every wire length. At λ/2 resonance both sin(2βL) and the denominator are small, so R ≈ Z₀/(αL) ≈ 3 000 Ω and X ≈ 0 — the antenna is nearly purely resistive and a 49:1 unun gives SWR ≈ 1.2:1. At λ/4 anti-resonance sin(2βL) = 0 again, so X = 0 and R ≈ Z₀·αL ≈ 34 Ω — matching directly with a 1:1 choke. Between resonances the large reactive component X drives SWR well above 2:1 through any practical transformer, so matching zones are naturally narrow (~±1.5 m around each resonance for 80m).

Harmonic-order correction: a wire operating at its nth harmonic resonance radiates more efficiently than a flat-α model predicts. The effective attenuation is reduced by: α_eff = α / √n_eff where n_eff = max(1, 2L / λ_eff). At the fundamental (n_eff = 1) α is unchanged. At the 2λ resonance (n_eff = 4, e.g. 10 m on a 40 m wire) α is halved, roughly doubling R_peak from ~750 Ω to ~1 500 Ω — consistent with real EFHW measurements at higher harmonics.

Parameters: Z₀ = 450 Ω (characteristic impedance, typical for HF wire near ground). α₀ = calibrated per-band so R_peak ≈ 3 000 Ω at each band's fundamental λ/2. R_min clamped to 25 Ω; R_max clamped to 5 000 Ω — practical limits for real wires.

Why the impedance floor is 25 Ω

At anti-resonance (λ/4 wire lengths), current is maximum at the feed point, giving low impedance. A theoretically lossless wire would approach 0 Ω, but real wires at typical HF heights give roughly 20–60 Ω due to radiation resistance and conductor/ground losses. 25 Ω is a conservative educational floor that reflects real-world measurement data.

Velocity factor

Real wire is electrically shorter than its physical length — the RF wave travels at VF × c along the conductor, so resonant peaks shift left to VF × λ/2 physical length. Typical values: bare copper ~0.98, insulated antenna wire ~0.95–0.97. At VF = 0.975 the 40m EFHW resonance moves from 20.97 m (free-space) to ~20.4 m — matching real-world experience. The velocity factor slider applies equally to the main wire and any counterpoise.

Counterpoise effect

A counterpoise wire connects to the "cold" terminal of the transformer, providing an RF return path. In the model both wires are open-ended lossy TLs whose complex impedances add in series at the feedpoint: Z_feed = Z_wire(L_wire) + Z_cp(L_cp). A counterpoise at λ/4 contributes mainly resistive impedance (low, ~34 Ω) — little effect on a 49:1 match. One near λ/2 contributes high resistive + near-zero reactive impedance and raises the total feedpoint Z, which can pull it out of the matching zone. When enabled, dashed ghost curves show the wire-alone impedance for comparison.

Model limitations: This is a qualitative guide, not a precise predictor. Key simplifications: (1) No mutual coupling — the wire and counterpoise radiate into each other's near field; omitting this can produce ±50% errors in the combined value. (2) No ground interaction — a real counterpoise near or on the ground has capacitive coupling to soil that shifts its resonant length and lowers its effective characteristic impedance. (3) Same Z₀ and α for both conductors — the counterpoise may differ significantly if it is shorter, thicker, or near ground. Treat the counterpoise display as a qualitative guide; for quantitative results use a NEC2/4 simulator.

Matching efficiency — how much power reaches the antenna

The tooltip ~xx% figure is the total delivered-power efficiency: η_total = η_mismatch × η_transformer × η_tuner

Mismatch efficiency: η_mismatch = 1 − |Γ|² where Γ = (Za − 50) / (Za + 50) and Za is the impedance presented to the radio side of the transformer. Full complex arithmetic is used — reactance increases |Γ| even when |Z| looks numerically close to the reference.

Transformer efficiency is a fixed constant per zone based on measured ferrite-toroid insertion loss at HF: 1:1 choke — 100% (negligible loss); 9:1 Unun — 90%; 49:1 Unun — 90%. Real losses vary with power level, frequency, and core material.

Tuner efficiency (when an ATU is enabled) uses a stress-aware inductor-Q model. Base inductor ESR: Rs₀ = 2πf · L / Q with Q = 120. An L-network circulates extra reactive current when the impedance ratio is large; the circulating current stress factor is kI² = max(R_load, 50) / min(R_load, 50) (the mismatch ratio). Effective loss resistance: Rs_eff = Rs₀ × kI². Final efficiency: η_tuner = R_load / (R_load + Rs_eff), clamped to [30%, 98%]. The tooltip shows "tuner eff xx% Q≈Y.Y" — a low Q means an easy match with little circulating current; a high Q (e.g. 5–8) means a hard match and noticeably more heat. Capacitor ESR is not modelled. When no ATU is active, η_tuner = 100%.

Radiation efficiency is not modelled. The percentage shown describes power delivered to the feedpoint, not power radiated. Radiation efficiency depends on radiation resistance vs total loss resistance (conductor + ground + near-field) and varies enormously with installation. At EFHW resonance (49:1 zone) a well-installed wire is typically 70–90% efficient; at λ/4 anti-resonance (1:1 zone, ~25–50 Ω) radiation resistance is only ~10–20 Ω — comparable to losses — giving 30–60% despite a near-perfect impedance match.

ATU L-network solver

When an Antenna Tuner is enabled, the tool runs an analytic L-network solver at each point in the tooltip. Two topologies are tried in order: series→shunt (series element facing the antenna, shunt element to ground) and shunt→series. The winning topology, inductance (µH), and capacitance (pF) are shown in the tooltip, along with the inductor-Q efficiency estimate. If neither topology can reach a match within the preset's Lmax/Cmax limits, "No match within tuner limits" is shown and the zone does not light up.

ATU presets — Internal (3:1): Lmax 4 µH, Cmax 500 pF; External (10:1): Lmax 24 µH, Cmax 1 200 pF; Wide-range (20:1): Lmax 60 µH, Cmax 3 500 pF. A Custom mode lets you set all three parameters manually.

Sweet Spots overlay

The ★ Sweet Spots toggle highlights wire lengths where a minimum number of active bands simultaneously fall inside matching zones. A gold column appears wherever the condition is met; a ★ marks the centre of each cluster. The min bands control sets the threshold — it auto-defaults to the number of active bands, requiring all selected bands to coincide simultaneously.

This is particularly useful for identifying a single wire length that covers multiple bands without retuning — the classic goal of a multi-band EFHW. Note that sweet spots depend on which zones are toggled on: enabling only the 49:1 Unun zone will show lengths where two or more bands are simultaneously near half-wave resonance, which is a strong multi-band indicator; enabling all zones will show a wider (but less selective) set of lengths.

ITU Region 1 allocation width

The ITU R1 alloc. width toggle scales each band's curve thickness proportionally to the ITU Region 1 amateur radio frequency allocation for that band, using a logarithmic scale from 1 px (60m — 15 kHz) to 4 px (6m — 2000 kHz). A wider line means more frequency room: e.g. 10m (1700 kHz) is nearly as thick as 6m, while 30m (50 kHz) and 17m/12m (100 kHz each) are visibly thinner. This gives a quick sense of operating flexibility at any given wire length.

How the matching zone ranges were derived

Zone overlay bands are dynamic — they grow or shrink based on tuner state and selected ATU preset:

1:1 Unun (25–100 Ω): A 1:1 unun (UNbalanced–UNbalanced) presents the same impedance on both ports — it does not transform the ratio, only suppresses common-mode RF current flowing back down the coax shield. In practice this is a current choke: a coil of coax, or coax threaded through ferrite cores (e.g. the W2DU choke). Because both the transmitter output and an end-fed wire are unbalanced, a unun is the correct term. It is commonly — and incorrectly — called a "1:1 balun"; a true balun (BALanced–UNbalanced) is only appropriate when one port is balanced, such as the centre of a dipole. A 50 Ω transceiver typically tolerates SWR ≤ 2:1, spanning 50 / 2 = 25 Ω to 50 × 2 = 100 Ω.

9:1 Unun (225–900 Ω without tuner): A 9:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to ~450 Ω at the antenna (zRef = 450 Ω). Without a tuner the overlay shows the natural SWR ≤ 2:1 window: 450 / 2 = 225 Ω to 450 × 2 = 900 Ω. When an ATU is active the overlay expands to the tuner preset's maxSWR (e.g. external 10:1 → ~45–4 500 Ω) reflecting the wider range the combined system can handle. Between wire resonances the feedpoint always has a large reactive component X, so complex SWR through the 9:1 is typically 7–15:1 even when |Z| ≈ 450 Ω — this is why the zone appears narrow without a tuner and why a counterpoise (3–7 m) that partially cancels the reactance is effectively part of the matching system.

49:1 Unun (1 225–4 900 Ω, shown as ~1 225–5 000 Ω): A 49:1 unun transforms 50 Ω to 50 × 49 = 2 450 Ω ideal (zRef). SWR ≤ 2:1 spans 2 450 / 2 = 1 225 Ω to 2 450 × 2 = 4 900 Ω. The overlay extends to the 5 000 Ω model ceiling, which is within measurement noise of the theoretical boundary. When a tuner is on the band widens to the preset's maxSWR, allowing the ATU to absorb residual mismatch at higher-order harmonics through the 49:1 zone.
